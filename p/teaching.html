The robotics course

Since September 2024 I've taught the robotics course at my school, where a group of students builds and programs a humanoid robot. It runs weekly and I plan the sessions, split the work between mechanics, electronics and software, and teach.

I got the course because I asked for it, not because anyone was looking for someone to run one. The students are a few years below me, which turns out to be the right distance: close enough that I remember not knowing what they don't know, far enough that they'll ask me things they wouldn't ask a teacher.

The bottleneck, and PibUi

We had one robot and a group of students, and the robot was available two afternoons a week. That meant most of the course's time went on waiting for a turn, and anyone who had an idea on a Wednesday evening had to hold onto it until the following week. Attendance suffered, and for a while I read that as a motivation problem.

It wasn't. It was a hardware access problem, and hardware access problems have software solutions.

PibUi is the control interface I wrote for the course. It's a Flask application with a Socket.IO layer for live control, handling individual servo control, calibration and limit setting, saving and replaying poses, and an emergency stop that cuts all servo output at once.

The part that changed the course is the mock mode: with no robot connected, the whole interface still runs and returns plausible responses, so a student can write, run and debug a movement sequence on a laptop at home and arrive on Tuesday with something that already works. The two afternoons stopped being the course and became the part of the course where you find out whether you were right.

The thing I'd change is the timing. The mock mode should have existed in the first month, not after I'd spent a term watching people wait.

MTG forscht.

My school runs selective classes for gifted students. One of my classmates is the strongest school-level mathematician in Germany and is on the national team for the International Mathematical Olympiad, and two others were in the selection group for it. There are thirty to forty students in these classes at any time.

At Jugend forscht, Germany's national science competition, we were almost absent, while schools nearby were entering six or seven teams a year. I assumed for a long time that this was about our students.

It isn't. Those schools have teachers with allocated hours for it, a budget for materials, and a structure that finds students in year 8 and walks them through a first project.

We had none of that, so our entries were whoever happened to organise themselves, which was almost nobody.

Roman Daugavet and I wrote a proposal and presented it to the school leadership, who approved it and took it to the parents' association, which provided a budget for project materials. The usual reason a school science project dies is that nobody wants to pay €200 for components. We asked Dr. Waschbüsch to supervise it, then went through the gifted classes from year 8 to year 13 in person and recruited two or three students from each, choosing people who were interested rather than people with the best marks. We designed the identity and printed the material ourselves, because something that looks like an official part of the school gets taken more seriously than a note on a noticeboard.

The first meeting was on 14 July 2026.

It is too early to say whether this works. I leave school in 2027, which means the structure has to keep running without either of us, and that is the actual test rather than the first year's results.
