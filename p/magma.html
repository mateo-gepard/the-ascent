MAGMA

A rover for volcanic terrain, with wheels that change diameter as the ground changes.

Role
Mechanical design — wheel mechanism and chassis
Team
Juan Rivera Chopinaud · Roman Daugavet
Brief
DLR Vulcano challenge — cross an active volcanic field, take measurements
Funding
Mouser Electronics · Stadtsparkasse München · Bayerischer Bildungsverband

Two incompatible problems.

Loose ash, where a narrow wheel sinks and a wide one floats. Broken rock, where a large wheel jams between edges and a smaller one steps through cleanly.

So the wheel changes size.

Curved segments on a gear ring slide outwards or inwards together, while the rover keeps moving.

What it is

A four-wheeled rover built for the German Aerospace Center's Vulcano challenge, which asks school teams to design a vehicle that can cross an active volcanic field and take useful environmental measurements.

Why it exists

The ground on a volcano is two incompatible problems at once. There is loose ash, where a narrow wheel sinks and a wide one floats. And there is broken rock, where a large wheel jams between edges and a smaller one steps through cleanly. A fixed wheel diameter is a compromise between the two, and every school rover I looked at accepted that compromise and then drove slowly enough to survive it.

We built wheels that change diameter instead. Each wheel opens and closes like a camera iris while the rover is moving, so it can run wide across ash and narrow across rock without stopping to be reconfigured.

How it works

Each wheel is built from overlapping curved segments carried on a gear ring. Rotating the ring relative to the hub slides every segment outwards or inwards at once, which changes the effective rolling diameter while keeping the contact surface continuous. The expansion drive is separate from the drive that turns the wheel, so diameter and speed are controlled independently and the rover doesn't have to stop to change configuration.

The load path was the difficult part. In a camera iris the blades carry nothing; here every segment carries a share of the vehicle's weight at whatever diameter it happens to be at, and the worst case is the fully expanded position, where the segments overlap least and the unsupported span is longest. That case set the segment thickness and the material, and it is also what sets the upper limit on the diameter range.

The starting point was a published variable-diameter wheel mechanism. I adapted it to a vehicle this size and to parts that could either be printed or bought from a normal distributor, because the version two bill of materials had to come in around €410. That budget was the real design constraint and it removed most of the elegant options early.

The rover carries an environmental sensor package and measures the air while it drives. The chassis was drawn to revision D before anything was cut.

What I got wrong

The rover was built. The submission wasn't.

Our five-minute demonstration video was due while I was in Georgia visiting my grandparents, and the two days before the deadline were the Bavarian state round of a different competition. The rover was at my flat, nobody was there to film it, and what I had were isolated five-second clips of individual functions, filmed in a hurry the evening before my flight. I cut them together and submitted them, and the result was bad. We had spent months and about €450 on a machine that worked, and what the judges saw was a badly shot clip of something that could have been anything.

Documentation is part of the build and has to be scheduled like the build, not treated as the thing you do at the end if there's time left.

On the next project I filmed each subsystem on the day it first worked.

Where it stands

Version one drives, the wheel mechanism works, and the sensor package is integrated. Version two is designed and partly sourced.

Autonomy isn't implemented; the current vehicle is remote-operated, and I'd rather say that than call it autonomous because the challenge brief uses the word.
