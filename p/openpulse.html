What it is

OpenPulse is a wearable built around interchangeable sensor modules.

You choose which signals you want to measure, twist the corresponding module into the housing, and the raw data goes to you rather than into a subscription service. We're building it for research groups, occupational safety and specialised monitoring, where people need the underlying signal rather than a score out of a hundred.

Why it exists

I wear a Whoop. It measures my heart rate, my skin temperature, my sleep and my recovery, and as a consumer device it is very good. What I can't do is get at the raw photoplethysmography signal, change what it measures, or use it at all if I stop paying. Every commercial wearable I looked at worked the same way: sealed hardware, a sensor set fixed for the life of the product, and data visible only through the manufacturer's app.

That is a sensible business model and a poor research instrument.

A sleep lab, a physiotherapist and a factory safety officer each need a different combination of sensors, and all three need the signal itself rather than an interpretation of it. So we designed the device around modules instead of a fixed board, and made the data format open from the start.

How it works

The architecture separates a mainboard from replaceable sensor pucks. The mainboard handles power, storage and Bluetooth Low Energy. Each puck carries its own sensors and identifies itself when connected, so the firmware knows what it is talking to and can configure itself accordingly.

The mechanical connection is a bayonet twist lock. We chose it over magnets, which release under a knock, and over screws, which need a tool and a free second hand. A bayonet gives a definite tactile end position, which matters when someone is changing a module on a wrist they can't see properly.

The current development hardware is one generation behind that, running a Seeed XIAO nRF52840 with a MAX30102, which is deliberate: it lets Roman work on firmware and signal processing while the final board is still moving.

The housing isn't drawn by hand. It's written as a CadQuery script, so wall thickness, module diameter and locking geometry are parameters rather than geometry. When a sensor changes, I change three numbers and regenerate every part instead of editing a model in a dozen places. That decision has saved more time than anything else in the project.

What I got wrong

We lost our first serious pitch.

In March 2026 we presented at the JUGEND GRÜNDET event at the Munich Chamber of Commerce and didn't place, because I pitched the idea instead of the device. We spent our three minutes explaining why closed ecosystems are a problem and almost none of it on the fact that there was a working board in a bag under the table. The jury concluded, reasonably, that we had a manifesto and no hardware.

After the results I stayed at our stand and put the actual board into the hands of anyone who stopped, including two of the jurors. One of them asked why we hadn't led with it. By the end of the evening we had a scholarship to the UnternehmerTUM Makerspace at TU Munich, which gave us the fabrication access we were actually short of, an offer to apply for a Bavarian innovation voucher, and two contacts we still use. Every pitch since then starts with the object.

The second thing I got wrong is visible in the hardware. We wrote a full five-sensor specification before we had a simple version working end to end, which is why the specification and the running board are still a generation apart. The board that exists is the one that taught us things. The board on paper taught us nothing until it was built.

Where it stands

A prototype mainboard has been manufactured and tested, the first thirty boards have come back from fabrication, and sensor modules are being assembled now. Wrist-worn prototypes are the goal for this summer.

The mobile app and the SDK are not finished. The signal-processing algorithms are specified but not validated against reference equipment, which means we can't make accuracy claims and don't make them. It is an actively developed prototype with awards attached, and it is not a certified medical device.
