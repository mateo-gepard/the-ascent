What it is

A wearable brass sundial, gold-plated, made by hand as a leaving present, and a small web tool that gives you the correction you need to read it accurately on any date and in any place.

Why it exists

A close friend was moving to the United States for a year and I wanted to make something rather than buy something. A sundial felt right for a year that was already measured out in advance, and making it wearable meant it would be carried rather than put on a shelf.

Then I worked out the error, and it stopped being a nice idea and became an engineering problem.

A sundial doesn't show clock time. It shows apparent solar time, and three separate effects sit between the two.

EffectCauseSize
Longitude Central European Time is defined at 15° east; Munich is at 11.6° east, and each degree is four minutes ≈ 14 min, constant
Equation of time The eccentricity of the Earth's orbit and the tilt of its axis −14 to +16 min
Daylight saving Bookkeeping. Nothing to do with the sun at all 60 min, or 0

Together those can put the object more than half an hour out, and the amount is different every week. I could have built a more accurate sundial. What I built instead was the correction, as a website, because the dial was going to a different latitude anyway and anything calibrated for Munich would have been wrong in a second way the moment it landed.

How it works

The tool takes a date and a location and returns the offset in minutes between what the dial reads and what a clock reads. The longitude correction is the difference between the local meridian and the time-zone meridian, at four minutes per degree. The equation of time is computed for the date. Daylight saving is applied by rule for the relevant jurisdiction. Latitude determines the angle the gnomon has to make with the horizontal, which is why a sundial that travels has to be adjusted rather than simply carried.

The object itself went through several versions before the one I gave away. Gold plating took considerably longer than I'd planned, which is why I finished the day before the flight rather than the week before, and it's the part of the process I'd do differently: I treated the finishing as a final step rather than as a process with its own failure modes and its own schedule.

What I documented

I kept a booklet alongside the build with every sketch, every calculation, the versions that didn't work, and a Polaroid of each stage. It exists because I wanted the person receiving it to see the work and not just the object.

It turned out to be the most useful thing I've ever done for my own engineering, because I can still reconstruct why every decision was made, including the ones I'd now make differently.

Where it stands

The sundial was given away in December 2025. The web tool is online and works for any location.

Photographs of the dial and pages from the booklet are being added.
