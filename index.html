What these have in common

In most of them the limitation turned out to be structural rather than personal. The robot in our course was only free two afternoons a week, so I wrote a simulator and the students could work anyway. A sundial is wrong by a different amount every week, so instead of building a more accurate one I wrote the correction. Every fitness tracker I could buy kept its raw signal behind a subscription, so we built one that doesn't.

I usually find the constraint more interesting than the result, and whatever I build to get around one tends to end up useful to everyone else stuck behind the same thing.

Research

Two months at a research reactor, finding out how strong a boron filament is.

Boron absorbs neutrons, which is why the reactor wants to 3D-print with it: you can then make shielding and beam-shaping parts in whatever shape an experiment needs, instead of machining them. Nobody had characterised how much load the printed material actually takes, so I printed specimens and pulled them apart on a universal testing machine.

It was the first time I worked to somebody else's measurement standard, and the first time I understood that a material property is a number you have to earn.

In public

Asking questions in rooms where students are usually there to listen.

Our school sent a delegation to a discussion on European deterrence, with the Inspector of the German Army, the Commanding General of US Army Europe and Africa, and a Bavarian state minister on the panel. Students were invited to ask questions. I asked mine.

I've also spoken on societal resilience alongside Lithuania's Consul General in Munich, and about AI in schools on Bavarian television.
